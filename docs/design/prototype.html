A proposal for collective self-government and renewal

The Garden

An effort to restore the possibility of self-government and enduring economic institutions to descendants of people enslaved in the United States.

The Garden proposes voluntary descendant-founded political communities, supported by an evidence-based historical census and a framework for negotiated public and commercial opportunities. It is a proposal for Congress—not an existing federal program.

The idea in one minute

A future rooted in history

Enslavement did not begin with an anonymous population. The people carried their own histories, families, institutions, languages and identities. The Garden asks what it would mean to respond not only to the loss of individual freedom, but also to the suppression of collective autonomy and opportunity.

Voluntary participation

Eligible descendants could choose whether to join new self-governing communities, without giving up United States citizenship. No one is enrolled by visiting this website.

Community decision-making

Participating communities would develop constitutions, elect institutions and make their own lawful governance and budget choices under the proposed framework.

Economic institutions

Conditional, negotiated opportunities could support collective enterprise. Outcomes would depend on law, agreements, business performance and accountability.

The proposed Act is a draft. Eligibility, jurisdiction, funding and recognition would require enacted law and legal review. Illustrative outcomes are not promises.

Why it matters

Lives larger than the records of captivity

Historical investigation should recover more than what was taken. It should restore visibility to political life, craftsmanship, agriculture, scholarship, family relationships, beliefs, trade, resistance and continuity wherever the evidence allows.

The historical atlas and the Garden proposal are distinct endeavors: research findings must stand on their evidence even when they complicate an argument for the policy.

Research that can be questioned

Records, interpretations and corrections should be linked and assigned clear review states. Uncertain identities and conflicting accounts must remain visible rather than being quietly smoothed into a compelling story.

Illustrations—including generated historical reconstructions—will require historical review and visible labels before publication. No unverified images are used in this prototype.

The proposed path

How would The Garden work?

These are proposed steps, not actions currently authorized by law.

  1. Congress considers a frameworkA bill would establish powers, oversight and conditions, subject to constitutional review.
  2. A historical census is assembledEvidence about relevant historical communities is published, corrected and independently assessed.
  3. People choose whether to participateEligible descendants may apply under the proposed criteria and choose voluntary affiliation.
  4. Communities establish institutionsConstitutions, elections and governing practices would be developed through lawful processes.
  5. States and nations negotiateQualified opportunities and agreements would depend on applicable law and voluntary arrangements.
  6. Independent accounting continuesFounding costs, enterprise income, repayment and public exposure would need transparent reporting.

Financing and public accountability

A recovery model, not a guarantee

The proposal aims to recover public founding expenditures through qualified enterprise income and defined repayment arrangements. That is an intention of the design, not evidence that repayments will certainly cover every public expenditure.

Transparent analysis must account for delayed income, unsuccessful ventures, public administration and costs that may never be recovered.

What about taxpayer cost?

The present legislative draft does not establish an unconditional zero-taxpayer-cost result. Its repayment and fiscal provisions contemplate public founding expenditures and the possibility of incomplete recovery.

A fiscal model should expose assumptions and independent review—not hide uncertainty behind a slogan.

Research access

Public evidence. Professional investigation.

Anyone should eventually be able to search the reviewed record, inspect the documents behind an assertion, and compare alternative readings without creating an account.

Advanced researchers will need full-text, geographic, temporal, language and provenance filters, as well as durable citations, downloads and reproducible queries.

Two separate environments

Open reading library: evidence cleared for public access and searchable without sign-in.

Restricted research workspace: collaborative transcription, annotations, corrections, source intake and peer review with independent rights and privacy safeguards.

These are target functions. This prototype contains no indexed corpus and no live search service.

Connection and participation

There should be many ways to take part

The public can ask questions, study the proposal, contribute evidence, help correct records or engage in policy discussion. Descendant and family contributors must be able to make informed choices about attribution, visibility and permitted use.

Contribute history

Future submission tools will separate research preservation from public display or promotional use. Family materials will not automatically become public.

Challenge an interpretation

Independent scholars and community historians should be able to submit corrections without endorsing the Garden legislation.

Discuss the proposal

Public questions and substantive objections belong alongside advocacy for the project. No visitor should be assigned a political view from ancestry or browsing behavior.

No forms in this prototype collect ancestry, documents, names, email addresses, or payment data.

Questions worth examining

Understanding also means questioning

Does this create an existing right to sovereignty?

No. It is draft federal legislation; recognition and the proposed powers would depend on Congress, constitutional scrutiny, implementing action, and applicable judicial review.

Is participation mandatory?

No. The draft describes voluntary participation and does not replace United States citizenship. The website does not confer eligibility, citizenship or enrollment.

Is the project guaranteed to cost taxpayers nothing?

No such guarantee is established by the current draft. It aims to recover public founding expenditures, but incomplete recovery and administration costs must be evaluated.

Must researchers agree with the policy?

No. Historical findings must be judged independently, including facts or objections that complicate an advocacy narrative.

Can someone submit family records privately?

The future research service is designed to offer privacy and consent controls. This prototype deliberately has no intake or enrollment function.

About this work

A proposal, a research project, and an invitation to examine both

The Garden aims to make its policy understandable while preserving the integrity of historical scholarship. This document is an early design study, not a deployed application, a live government service or a completed research edition. Important historical, legal, fiscal and security reviews remain unfinished.

For the draft legislation and complete development plan, consult the project’s controlled documents and repository. Source and permission checks must precede public archive publication.
